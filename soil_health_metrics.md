# Soil Health & Ecological Metrics

When you ship your pooled "Composite Soil" samples to an agronomy lab (such as Texas A&M AgriLife Extension or a commercial soil lab), you need to request specific test packages. 

## 1. Routine Soil Analysis (Physicochemical)
*This is the standard package that tells you if the plant has the building blocks it needs to grow and defend itself.*
*   **Soil pH:** Nightshades generally prefer slightly acidic to neutral soils. High alkalinity might stress them.
*   **Nitrogen (N) - Nitrate/Nitrite:** The most critical nutrient for plant growth and the primary building block for the alkaloids the plant uses for defense. 
*   **Phosphorus (P) & Potassium (K):** Essential for root development and drought tolerance.
*   **Organic Matter (%):** Highly correlated with healthy fungal networks and soil moisture retention. Disturbed/roadside soils usually have terrible organic matter.
*   **Salinity / Electrical Conductivity (EC):** Silverleaf nightshade is highly salt-tolerant. Checking this tells you if it is out-competing native plants purely because the soil is too salty for anything else to grow.

## 2. Nematode Analysis
*This requires a specialized test and the soil must be shipped fresh/cool, NOT dried out.*
*   **Plant-Parasitic Nematode Count:** Root-knot nematodes (Meloidogyne spp.) heavily attack nightshades. High nematode pressure below-ground might severely weaken the plant's ability to fight off the Texas Potato Beetle above-ground.

## 3. Soil Moisture (In-House / Lab)
*   **Gravimetric Water Content:** You can actually do this in your lab for free! Weigh a sample of wet soil straight out of the cooler. Bake it in your drying oven until bone dry. Weigh it again. The difference is the water weight. This gives you a precise "Drought Stress" metric to replace your visual field guess.

## 4. Fungal / Mycorrhizal Assays (Advanced)
*If your lab has the budget or the graduate student manpower.*
*   **AMF Spore Extraction:** Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi (AMF) form symbiotic relationships with plant roots. You can extract and count spores from the soil using wet sieving and decanting.
*   **Root Staining:** You can stain the roots you bring back from the field to visually check under a microscope if the fungi are actually colonizing the inside of the root tissues.
