## Statewide Expedition: Experimental Design

### The Core Gap in the Literature
Previous studies from your lab (Kariyat, Chavana, Kasper) heavily focused on **above-ground traits** (spines, leaf damage, and adult insect presence). The massive, gaping hole in the literature is the **Above-Ground/Below-Ground Interaction**. No one has mapped how below-ground fungal networks (or disruption to them) impact the plant's ability to defend itself against the Texas Potato Beetle across an entire state.

### Defining Your Variables
*   **Independent Variables (What you compare):** 
    *   **Geography:** Ecoregion (Valley vs. Panhandle vs. Coastal Plains).
    *   **Treatment:** Disturbed soils (urban, mowed, roadside) vs. Undisturbed soils (wild pastures).
*   **Dependent Variables (What you measure):**
    *   **Above-Ground:** Plant height, spine density, insect life-stage counts (Eggs vs. Larvae vs. Adults), defoliation percentage.
    *   **Below-Ground:** Root/rhizome biomass, presence/absence of Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi (AMF) or fungal rot.
*   **The Control:**
    *   Your "Baseline Control" will be the **Undisturbed, native populations** with minimal insect damage. You compare everything else (urban plants, heavily defoliated plants) back to these baseline plants to see how they deviate.

## Novel Hypotheses to Test on the Trip

1. **The Fungal Disruption Hypothesis (Highly Novel):** 
    *   *Concept:* Silverleaf nightshade relies on its deep, massive rhizome root system to survive. If urban/disturbed soils disrupt the native fungal networks (mycorrhizae) attached to these roots, the plant becomes biochemically weaker.
    *   *Hypothesis:* Plants pulled from disturbed soils will have disrupted root fungi and, consequently, support significantly higher insect biomass (more larvae and eggs) because they cannot synthesize chemical defenses as effectively as undisturbed plants.

2. **Life-Stage vs. Fecundity Breakdown:** 
    *   *Concept:* Counting just "adult bugs" doesn't tell the whole story.
    *   *Hypothesis:* Drought-stressed plants in West Texas will show high numbers of *adults*, but low *fecundity* (eggs/larvae) because the plant lacks the nitrogen required for insect reproduction. Coastal plants will show the opposite.

3. **The Spines vs. Fungi Trade-off:**
    *   *Concept:* Plants have a limited energy budget.
    *   *Hypothesis:* Plants investing heavily in below-ground fungal networks and root biomass will have lower above-ground mechanical defenses (fewer internode spines).

4. **The "Enemy Release" and Genetic Origin Hypothesis:**
    *   *Concept:* If the Rio Grande Valley is the true evolutionary origin of Silverleaf Nightshade, the populations there should have the highest genetic diversity. As the plant invaded northward/westward, it went through a "genetic bottleneck" and escaped its native predators.
    *   *Hypothesis:* Leaf DNA sequencing will show maximum genetic diversity in Hidalgo County. Furthermore, these Southern origin plants will have higher baseline defenses (spines/alkaloids) because they evolved alongside specific predators, whereas Panhandle plants will have lower genetic diversity and weaker defenses (Enemy Release).

5. **Multi-Trophic Biocontrol (Nematodes vs. Beetles):**
    *   *Concept:* Below-ground herbivores (Nematodes) weaken the plant systemically. 
    *   *Hypothesis:* There is a synergistic effect between biocontrol agents. Plants suffering from severe root-knot nematode damage (confirmed via soil testing) will be significantly more susceptible to extreme defoliation by Texas Potato Beetles because the plant lacks the root energy to synthesize alkaloids.
